Big Idea

Use non-standard billiards to understand how tiny imperfections can cause chaotic amplification — and connect that to unexpected heating in trapped-ion quantum computers.

Why this matters (The "So What?")

This isn’t just a math puzzle. If we understand how geometry and chaos control noise, we can design better quantum devices.

1. Better intuition for noise

Noise in quantum devices is often treated as a black box. Billiards let us build geometric intuition for when instability and heating will appear.

2. Design rules for hardware

If certain shapes and configurations are inherently more stable, that’s a design rule. We can use math to rule out bad geometries before spending time and money in the lab.

3. Cross-disciplinary playground

This project sits at the intersection of math, physics, CS, and stats. There is room for simulations, proofs, data analysis, and software.

The Basics: What are we actually talking about?

What is a qubit?

A qubit is like a bit, but it can be in a combination of |0⟩ and |1⟩. You can picture it as a point on a sphere (the Bloch sphere).

What is "noise"?

Noise is any random disturbance (stray fields, roughness) that nudges the quantum state away from where it’s supposed to be.

What is chaos?

Chaos is when a system follows strict rules, but tiny changes in initial conditions explode over time.

What to look for

  • Zero Roughness: The path is predictable. It remembers where it came from.
  • High Roughness: Every bounce adds a random kick. The path fills the space chaotically.

This "chaotic heating" in the classical world maps onto anomalous heating in the quantum world.

Simulation Bloch Sphere Representation

What is happening?

  • The Arrow: Represents the state of the qubit. Up is |0⟩, Down is |1⟩.
  • The Motion: Ideally, we apply a pulse to rotate it smoothly (Rabi oscillation).
  • The Noise: As you increase noise, the arrow jitters. In a real experiment, this destroys the information (decoherence).

My thesis explores how the geometric chaos from Demo 1 causes the state noise you see here in Demo 2.
