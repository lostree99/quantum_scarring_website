Start here: what’s going on?

No physics degree required. If you’ve done a bit of calculus and linear algebra, you’re absolutely fine. Even if you haven’t, you can still follow the story.

What is a qubit?

A qubit is like a bit, but it can be in a combination of |0⟩ and |1⟩ at the same time. You can picture it as a point on a sphere (the Bloch sphere) instead of a simple 0/1 toggle.

The catch: qubits are fragile. The environment constantly tries to mess them up.

What is “noise”?

Noise is any random disturbance that nudges the quantum state away from where it’s supposed to be. Stray electric fields, surface roughness, vibrations — they all show up as errors in our quantum logic.

In trapped-ion devices, ions can mysteriously “heat up” even when we think we’ve shielded everything. That’s called anomalous heating.

What is chaos?

Chaos isn’t just “randomness.” It’s when a system follows strict deterministic rules, but tiny changes in initial conditions explode over time.

Billiards — literally a ball bouncing in a shaped table — are a classic playground for chaos. Change the shape a tiny bit, and everything changes.

The big question: Can we use the math of chaotic billiards to understand and tame noise in quantum hardware?

What this is actually showing

  • We launch a single “ray” (think: an ion’s trajectory) inside a rectangular trap and let it bounce specularly off the walls.
  • With zero roughness, reflections are perfect: the trajectory is structured and remembers its initial direction.
  • As you increase roughness, each bounce picks up a tiny random angle kick — like hitting a slightly rough or micro-structured electrode.

Over many bounces, those tiny kicks add up and the path starts to look chaotic and space-filling. This is the same story as anomalous heating: small geometric / surface features can dramatically change stability and how energy is absorbed.

My thesis in one picture and a few sentences

The short version: I use non-standard billiards as a clean mathematical playground to study how small geometric imperfections can lead to chaotic amplification, and then connect that behavior to anomalous heating in trapped-ion quantum computers.

1. Non-standard billiards

Instead of nice, symmetric tables, I look at:

  • Billiards with gravity
  • Micro-structured or “rough” walls
  • Weird aspect ratios and shapes

These aren’t just pretty pictures — they push us into regimes where standard textbook theory doesn’t cover everything.

2. Stability & chaos

I study how trajectories behave using:

  • Monodromy matrices
  • Lyapunov exponents
  • Curvature and differential geometry

The key object: how nearby trajectories separate or stay close when you follow them around periodic orbits.

3. Connection to ion traps

Trapped ions can “heat up” due to uncontrolled electric-field noise from surfaces. I look at how the geometry of the system controls the sensitivity to those disturbances.

Billiards give a mathematically precise way to ask: “If I change this geometric feature, how does stability change?”

One line summary: Geometry → dynamics → chaos → noise. Use billiards to understand where quantum hardware is fragile — and how to make it more robust.

Why this matters (and why you should care)

This isn’t just a math puzzle. If we understand how geometry and chaos control noise, we can design better quantum devices.

Better intuition for noise

Noise in quantum devices is often treated as a black box. Billiards let us build geometric intuition for when instability and heating will appear.

Design rules for hardware

If certain shapes and configurations are inherently more stable, that’s a design rule. We can use math to rule out bad geometries before spending time and money in the lab.

Quick reality check: tiny quantum quiz

Click “Show answer” and argue with your friends later.

Q1. Do quantum computers replace all classical computers?

A) Yes, they’re strictly more powerful
B) No, they’re good for specific types of problems
C) They’re literally magic

Mostly B. Quantum computers are powerful for certain structured problems (like factoring, some simulation tasks), but classical computers are not going anywhere. They complement each other.

Q2. Is chaos the same as randomness?

A) Yes
B) No, chaos is deterministic but sensitive
C) Only at full moon

B. Chaotic systems follow precise rules, but tiny differences in initial conditions explode over time. That’s different from pure randomness.

Q3. Why care about tiny geometric imperfections?

A) They’re aesthetic only
B) They can drastically change stability and heating
C) They only matter in pure math

B. In both billiards and trapped ions, small geometric imperfections can be the difference between stable and noisy behavior.
